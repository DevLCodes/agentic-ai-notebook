Confidence Scoring

An answer with a number next to it is only useful if the number means something, and if something happens because of it. Confidence is a decision input: answer, hedge, hand it to a human, or try a bigger model. It is not a badge for the UI.

1 · Signals, then one number

No single signal is enough. Each one measures something different, and each can be fooled.

signalwhat it measureswhat fools it
Top reranker scorehow well the best chunk matches the question (cross-encoder)a confident match to the wrong year’s filing
Margin #1 − #2whether one chunk clearly wins, or several near-ties competetwo copies of the same table look like a tie
Chunks above a thresholdhow much evidence there isboilerplate that repeats in every filing

Token logprobshow likely the model finds its own wordingfluent, confident wording of a wrong fact. Logprobs measure the text, not the truth
Self-consistencywhether N sampled answers agree (section 2)a model that is consistently wrong
Verbalised confidencethe model saying “I’m 90% sure”it is known to be overconfident; treat it as weak evidence

Tool outcomethe calculator’s result matches the XBRL valuehard to fool; good as a hard rule
Query signalsout of scope (“should I buy AAPL?”), ambiguous (“margin”)a router that was never tested on rephrasings

Lab: combine the signals

The scorer is a small logistic regression trained on a few hundred labelled answers, so its output is a probability that the answer is right. Hard rules run first. Move the sliders.

A hand-weighted rule (“average the scores”) is the usual first version. It is fine as a starting point, but the output is not a probability and the weights are guesses. Once you have labelled outcomes, fit the weights: a logistic regression has a handful of coefficients, trains in milliseconds, and the coefficients show you which signals actually predict correctness.


  
  
2 · Ask again: self-consistency

  
Sample the same question five times at a temperature above zero. If the model knows the answer, the samples agree. If it is guessing, they scatter.

  

    

    

    

    

      
Exact-string agreement

      
Agreement by meaning

      
Semantic entropy (bits)

    

    

  

  
Group the samples by meaning, not by string: “29.8%” and “about 30% (29.8%)” are the same answer. Entropy over those meaning clusters is semantic entropy (Farquhar et al., Nature, 2024). It is 0 when every sample means the same thing and log₂ 5 ≈ 2.32 when all five differ. The cost is N model calls per question, so keep it for answers where a mistake is expensive, or run it on a sample.


  
  
3 · A score is not a probability until you calibrate it

  
A cosine similarity of 0.83 or a rerank logit of 7.2 orders results. Neither one means “83% likely to be right”. Calibrated means that of all answers scored 0.7, about 70% are correct.

  

    

      

      
      
Reliability diagram: 200 FinSight answers in 5 bins of the raw score. Dots on the dashed line are perfectly calibrated; red lines are the gaps.

    

    

      
Expected Calibration Error: 

      

      

    

  

  
ECE is the average gap between confidence and accuracy, weighted by how many answers fall in each bin: ECE = Σ (nᵢ / N) · |accᵢ − confᵢ|.
    Three common fixes, all fitted on a held-out labelled set, never on the data you report:

  
    
    
  • Temperature scaling: divide the logit by one number T. It keeps the ranking and can only squeeze or stretch scores around 50%.
    • 
    
  • Platt scaling: a one-feature logistic regression, p = σ(a · logit(s) + b). The bias term b lets it shift scores down, which is what an overconfident system needs.
    • 
    
  • Isotonic regression: a monotone step function. It is the most flexible, but it needs more data (roughly a thousand labels or more) and overfits small sets.
    •

This is selective prediction: you trade coverage for accuracy. The right point depends on what a wrong answer costs compared with a human’s time. A wrong revenue figure in an analyst’s model costs far more than a wrong page number. Three refinements:

  
    
    
  • A hedge band between “answer” and “escalate”, where the answer ships with its caveat and sources.
    • 
    
  • Per-segment thresholds: numeric questions, open-ended summaries and out-of-scope requests each get their own threshold, chosen on their own labelled slice.
    • 
    
  • A hard rule beats a score: a calculator mismatch or a failed guardrail escalates whatever the blend says.
    • 
  

  
Watching it in production

  
Track the escalation rate per day and per segment. A sudden drop after a new embedding model, reranker or index usually means the score distribution moved, not that the system got better. Re-fit the calibration on the labelled set after every model or index change, and feed analyst decisions from the queue back in as new labels (see evals and Testing & Evals).


  

    

      Remember
      
    
        
  • Confidence exists to drive a decision: answer, hedge, escalate or route.
    • 
        
  • Combine several signals with a model fitted on labelled outcomes.
    • 
        
  • Calibrate on held-out data and report ECE; re-fit after any model or index change.
    • 
        
  • Choose thresholds from costs, per segment, and let hard rules override.
    •
