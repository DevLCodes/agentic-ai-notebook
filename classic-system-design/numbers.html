Why estimate

Estimates decide the design. 50 writes a second fits on one Postgres node; 500,000 does not. You only need to be right to within a factor of ten.

Round hard, say your assumptions out loud, and use the result to pick components. Interviewers care much more about the reasoning than the exact number.

The pattern to remember: memory is roughly 1,000× faster than an SSD random read; a network round trip inside a region is about 10× an SSD read; and a trip across a continent is about 100× a round trip inside one region. Every layer you skip is a win.

Availability

AvailabilityDowntime per yearPer month
99% (two nines)~3.65 days~7.3 hours
99.9% (three nines)~8.8 hours~44 minutes
99.99% (four nines)~53 minutes~4.4 minutes
99.999% (five nines)~5.3 minutes~26 seconds

Dependencies in series multiply: three services at 99.9% each give about 99.7% together. Redundant copies in parallel improve it: two independent 99% replicas give 99.99%, if their failures really are independent.

Try it: size a service

Fill in a few product assumptions and get the numbers that shape the design.

Storage counts three replicas for the database and ignores compression and indexes. Read bandwidth assumes each read returns one item plus any media.

The two-minute method

  1. Users and actions. Daily actives, and what a typical user does per day.
  2. Requests per second. Actions per day ÷ 100,000, then × a peak factor. Split reads and writes; the ratio picks your caching and replication strategy.
  3. Storage. Writes per day × size × 365 × years, × replication. Separate small records (database) from blobs (object storage).
  4. Bandwidth. Requests per second × response size. Media usually dominates and goes through a CDN.
  5. Memory for cache. Cache the hot 10–20% of a day's reads; check it fits in a few nodes.
  6. Turn numbers into decisions. "40k peak reads/s means a cache and replicas; 1k writes/s fits one leader; 200 TB of photos means S3 and a CDN."
