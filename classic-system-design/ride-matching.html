Ride Matching

Design the matching system for a ride-hailing app. Drivers' phones report their location every few seconds; a rider asks for a ride and should be matched with a nearby driver who accepts within seconds, at a price that reflects demand. Plan for 20 million rides a day and 5 million drivers online at peak, across hundreds of cities.

Requirements

  • Clarify

Two very different workloads meet here: a firehose of location updates, and a much smaller stream of ride requests that each need a fast, correct, one-driver-only decision.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Location updates: 5M drivers ÷ 4 s = 1.25M writes a second. Each is ~100 bytes (driver ID, lat, lng, heading, speed, timestamp, status), so ~125 MB/s coming in.
  • Live index: only the latest point per driver matters: 5M × ~100 B ≈ 500 MB in total, and far less per city. It fits in memory on a handful of machines.
  • History: keeping every point is 1.25M × 100 B × 86,400 ≈ 10 TB a day raw. Trips need their route (for fares and disputes), so keep trip points in full, compress them, and sample idle driving.
  • Requests: 1,000 a second at peak, each comparing 20–50 candidate drivers by road ETA: ~50,000 ETA calculations a second, which is why ETAs use precomputed road graphs.

Architecture

  • Whole system
  • Location service receives pings over persistent connections, updates the in-memory geo index for the driver's city, and streams the points to Kafka for trip routes, analytics and surge.
  • Ride service issues quotes (with the pricing service) and creates ride requests.
  • Dispatch finds nearby available drivers, ranks them by ETA from the ETA service, offers the ride, and on acceptance flips the driver's state atomically in the driver state store.
  • Trip service owns the ride's lifecycle from assignment to drop-off and fare.

Finding nearby drivers

  • Deep dive 1
  • Partitioning

"Who's within 2 km of this point?" must be answered in milliseconds over a set of drivers that changes 1.25 million times a second.

Scanning every driver in a city and computing distances is too slow, and a database range query on latitude and longitude can't use one index for both. The answer is to divide the map into cells, so a location becomes a cell ID and "nearby" becomes "these few cells".

IndexHowTrade-off
GeohashInterleave lat/lng bits into a string; a shared prefix means nearbySimple and works in any key-value store (Redis GEOADD/GEOSEARCH use it). Cells are rectangles that distort with latitude, and neighbours across a cell edge can have very different prefixes
QuadtreeSplit a region into four until each leaf holds at most N driversAdapts to density (small cells downtown, big ones in suburbs); rebalancing as drivers move is fiddly
H3 hexagonsUber's hierarchical grid of hexagons at fixed resolutionsEvery neighbour is the same distance away, and "rings" around a cell are cheap to enumerate. Fixed resolution, so pick it per city

I'd use H3 at resolution 8 or 9 (cells ~0.7 km² or ~0.1 km²). The index is a map from cell ID to the set of available drivers in it. To search, take the rider's cell plus k rings of neighbours (1, 7, 19, 37 cells for k = 0–3), read those sets, and widen k until there are enough candidates. Then compute exact distances only for those few dozen drivers.

Only available drivers are in the searchable sets. A driver on a trip is in a separate structure (still tracked, but not offered), and drivers who stop pinging drop out after a short TTL.

Try it: how big a search area?

  • Lab

Pick how dense available drivers are, the H3 cell size, and how many rings to search. You want enough candidates to choose a good driver, but not so many that you waste ETA calculations.

Cell size

Illustrative model: average H3 cell areas; cells searched = 1 + 3k(k+1); expected candidates = density × area; radius is that of a circle with the same area. Real densities vary block by block, which is why dispatch widens the search adaptively.

A million location updates a second

  • Scale
  • Streams
  • Shard by city. Every update and every search for a city goes to the same few location servers, which hold that city's index in memory. A big city (hundreds of thousands of drivers) is split further by region of the map. No query ever needs data from two cities.
  • Update cheaply. On each ping, compute the H3 cell. If it's the same cell as last time (most pings), just update the driver's record. If it changed, remove the driver from the old cell's set and add them to the new one. That's two set operations, in memory.
  • Lossy is fine. If a ping is dropped, the next one arrives in 4 seconds. Don't queue, retry or persist live pings synchronously; only the newest point matters, so an older point arriving late is discarded by timestamp.
  • Survive a node loss. The index is rebuildable: replicas of each city shard receive the same stream, and a fresh node fills up from pings within one ping interval. Losing a shard means a few seconds of thinner search results, not lost data.
  • Persist asynchronously. The same pings go to Kafka, where consumers write trip routes (in full, for fares and disputes), feed surge pricing, and keep sampled history for ETA model training.

Adaptive ping rate. An idle driver parked for ten minutes doesn't need to ping every 4 seconds, and one moving fast on a highway might ping more often. Letting the server tell the app how often to report cuts load and battery use without making the index stale where it matters.

Dispatch: offer, accept, never double-book

  • Deep dive 2
  • Consistency

Finding candidates is half the job. The other half is offering the ride to one driver at a time, handling silence and refusals, and making sure two riders never get the same driver.

  1. Rank candidates by road ETA to the pickup (not straight-line distance: the closest driver may be across a river), adjusted for heading, driver rating and fairness to drivers who've waited longest.
  2. Reserve, then offer. Atomically move the best driver from available to offered:r_909 with an expiry: UPDATE drivers SET state='offered', ride=$r, offer_expires=now()+15s WHERE id=$d AND state='available' (or a Redis Lua script / SET NX with TTL). If zero rows change, someone else got them; take the next candidate.
  3. Push the offer to the driver's app with a 15-second countdown.
  4. Accept flips offered:r_909 → assigned:r_909 with the same guarded update, and the trip service tells the rider. A late accept after expiry fails the guard and the driver sees "this ride is no longer available".
  5. Decline or silence: the reservation expires (or is released) and dispatch offers the next candidate. After a few misses, widen the search.

The guarded compare-and-set on the driver's state is what prevents double-booking. It doesn't matter how many dispatchers race for the same driver: only one update can succeed.

Greedy or batched?

Matching each request the moment it arrives (greedy) is simple, but can make poor choices for the city as a whole: it gives the only nearby driver to a rider who had another option a minute away. Batched matching collects requests for 1–2 seconds per area and solves an assignment problem (minimise total pickup time across all riders and drivers in the batch). It adds a second of latency for noticeably better ETAs at busy times. Many systems switch to batching only in dense areas at peak.

ETAs and routing

  • Quality

Dispatch needs 20–50 pickup ETAs per request within a few milliseconds each. Shortest-path search over a city's road graph from scratch (Dijkstra) is too slow at 50,000 a second, so the routing engine uses precomputed graphs (contraction hierarchies or similar) that answer point-to-point queries in about a millisecond, with live traffic applied as edge weights updated every few minutes.

On top of the graph estimate, an ML model corrects ETAs from historical trips (time of day, weather, events, pickup spot quirks like airport ramps). For ranking candidates, a slightly stale ETA is fine. For what the rider sees, use the better estimate.

Surge pricing

  • Marketplace

Every minute or so, a stream job reads requests and available drivers per H3 cell (coarser, say resolution 7) from Kafka and computes a demand-to-supply ratio. Where demand outstrips supply, a multiplier raises the price, which both damps demand and draws drivers in. Smooth the multiplier across neighbouring cells and over time, so prices don't jump block to block or second to second, and cap it, with stricter caps during emergencies.

The quote captures the multiplier at request time, so the rider pays the price they agreed to even if surge changes before pickup.

The trip

  • State

The trip service owns a state machine: MATCHING → DRIVER_ASSIGNED → DRIVER_ARRIVED → ON_TRIP → COMPLETED, with CANCELLED branches carrying who cancelled and when (for fees). Each transition is a guarded update in a relational database (one row per trip), and an event to Kafka for payments, receipts and analytics. While the trip is active, the rider's app receives the driver's position from the location stream (a few times a second during the final approach), and the route points are stored for fare calculation and disputes.

Payment runs at the end through the payments system: authorise at request, capture at completion.

Deployment and cities

  • Production
  • Load balancing

Cities are assigned to regions close to them, and each city's location, dispatch and pricing shards run in that region. That keeps latency low, and an incident in one region affects only its cities. Rider and trip records replicate to a backup region; the live geo index doesn't need to, because it rebuilds from pings within seconds.

Launch a new city without a deploy. City boundaries, H3 resolution, surge caps and product types are configuration. A new city is a config change plus shard assignment, rather than code.

Roll out dispatch changes city by city with experiments, watching match rate, pickup ETAs and cancellations. Matching algorithms are compared with switchback tests (alternating the algorithm by time window in a city), because riders and drivers in one city affect each other and can't be split cleanly into A and B.

"Driver pings arrive at 1.25 million a second, so the live index lives in memory, sharded by city: each ping maps to an H3 cell, and only drivers who change cell cost two set operations. Pings also go to Kafka for trip routes and surge. A ride request gets a locked quote, then dispatch reads the rider's cell and a few rings of neighbours, widens until it has enough candidates, and ranks them by road ETA from precomputed graphs. It reserves the best driver with a compare-and-set from available to offered with a 15-second expiry, pushes the offer, and on accept flips to assigned. A race means a failed update and the next candidate, so no driver is ever double-booked. At peak in dense areas, batching requests for a second gives better matches overall."
