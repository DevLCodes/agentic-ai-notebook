Reranking with Cross-Encoders

A second, more careful pass that reads the question and each candidate together, so the few chunks that actually answer it reach the prompt.

Why rerank

Retrieval is tuned for recall: it casts a wide net quickly. Reranking is tuned for precision: it reads each candidate carefully and puts the best few on top.

The prompt only holds a handful of chunks, and models pay most attention to the first ones. Getting the right chunk into position 1 instead of position 7 is often the cheapest quality win in a RAG system.

Bi-encoders vs cross-encoders

Both start from the same kind of transformer. The difference is when the question and the chunk meet.

Bi-encoderCross-encoder
InputQuery and chunk separatelyQuery and chunk concatenated
OutputTwo vectors, compared by cosine or dot productOne relevance score (a logit)
InteractionNone until the end: the chunk is squeezed into ~384–1,024 numbers firstToken-level: “2023” in the question attends to “2022” in the chunk and notices they differ
PrecomputeYes: embed the corpus once, then ANN search in millisecondsNo: the score depends on the query, so the model runs at query time
Cost per queryOne forward pass plus an index lookupOne forward pass per candidate: O(N)
JobRecall: find 50–100 plausible chunks among millionsPrecision: put the right 5 at the top

The cross-encoder is more accurate for the same reason it is expensive: it never compresses the chunk before seeing the question. A bi-encoder must decide what a chunk is “about” without knowing the question, so chunks that share vocabulary (“operating margin”, “gross margin”, a table-of-contents line) land close together. Scoring a whole corpus with a cross-encoder is out of the question: 50,000 chunks would mean 50,000 transformer passes per query.

Where it sits

After reranking, apply cheap business rules: boost recent and authoritative sources, demote archived ones, and collapse several chunks from one document so the top results are not all copies of the same page. The weights behind those boosts are covered in weights in retrieval.

The latency budget

A reranker costs a fixed price per pair. The price grows with the number of candidates and the tokens in each pair, and the whole call sits on the critical path before the LLM can start.

rerank_ms ≈ candidates × (tokens_per_pair / 512) ÷ pairs_per_second_at_512 × 1000
  • Rerank fewer. Measure first-stage recall@k. If recall@50 ≈ recall@100, send 50.
  • Shorter pairs. Rerank the chunk, not the parent section, and truncate to the model’s trained length (often 512 tokens).
  • Batch on a GPU. Send all pairs for one query in one or a few batches; padding to similar lengths helps.
  • A smaller or distilled model. A 22M-parameter MiniLM is many times cheaper than the 568M-parameter bge-reranker-v2-m3.
  • Skip when easy. If the first stage is confident (an exact ID match, one dominant hit), don’t rerank; let the router decide.

Try it: size the rerank

Model

Throughputs are rough planning numbers, not benchmarks; measure on your own hardware, batch size and chunk length. Cost is modelled as linear in tokens, but attention is quadratic, so long pairs are worse than shown. A hosted API (Cohere, Voyage, Jina) replaces this with one network call, typically 100–300 ms for about 100 short documents.

Scores are not probabilities

A cross-encoder outputs a raw logit. Libraries often pass it through a sigmoid so it lands in 0–1, but it was trained to order chunks for one query, not to mean “73% likely to be relevant” across queries. Easy questions score everything high; oddly phrased ones score even the right chunk low.

A fixed cut-off such as “drop below 0.7” behaves differently per query type, model and chunk length. If you need a threshold (to say “I couldn’t find this”), pick it on your own labelled set and re-pick it whenever the model changes. Confidence scoring covers calibration.

ColBERT and LLM rerankers

Late interaction: ColBERT’s MaxSim

score(q, d) = Σ over query tokens qᵢ of max over doc tokens dⱼ of (qᵢ · dⱼ)

ColBERT keeps one small vector per token instead of one per chunk. Chunk token vectors are precomputed like a bi-encoder; at query time each query token finds its best-matching chunk token, and the score is the sum of those maxima. You get token-level matching without a transformer pass per pair, at the cost of a much larger index (ColBERTv2 compresses it with residuals).

LLM rerankers

StyleHow it worksTrade-off
PointwiseScore each (query, chunk) alone, yes/no or 0–3; monoT5 is the classic fine-tuned versionParallel and simple; scores drift between calls
Pairwise“Which of these two answers better?”, then aggregate wins (duoT5)Robust judgements, but O(N²) comparisons unless you sort cleverly
ListwiseShow 20 passages, ask for ranked IDs, slide a window over 100 (RankGPT)Sees candidates side by side; long prompts, order-sensitive, slowest

An LLM reranker can follow instructions (“prefer audited statements over press releases”) that a cross-encoder can’t. It also costs 10–100× the latency. Treat it as a candidate to beat, not the default.

How rerankers are trained

  • MS MARCO: Bing queries over ~8.8 million passages with human-clicked positives. Most open cross-encoders (the ms-marco MiniLM family) start here.
  • Hard negatives: training negatives are chunks the retriever ranked highly but are wrong. Random negatives teach nothing.
  • Distillation: a big teacher (a large cross-encoder or an LLM) labels pairs; a small student learns its scores. The same trick trains better bi-encoders.
  • Domain fine-tuning: a few thousand (question, good chunk, hard negative) triples from your logs often beat a bigger general model on filings, contracts or code.

Measure it, or skip it

Run the same golden set twice, with and without the reranker, and compare NDCG@10 and MRR (see evals). Also log first-stage recall@k: if the right chunk isn’t in the candidates, no reranker can rescue it. Fix recall with hybrid search, better embeddings or chunking, then rerank.

  • Skip it for tiny corpora where the top 5 are already right on your golden set.
  • Skip it under strict latency budgets where 200 ms matters more than the gain; measure first.
  • Skip it for exact lookups (an ID search) where there is only one right answer.

Saying it in an interview

“Retrieval is a two-stage funnel. Hybrid search pulls the top 100 for recall, then a cross-encoder reranks them and we keep 5 to 8 for the prompt. I’d start with a hosted reranker or bge-reranker on a GPU, budget about 150 ms at p95, and trim candidates to where first-stage recall flattens. I wouldn’t threshold raw reranker scores without calibrating on our own labels, and I’d prove the reranker earns its latency with NDCG@10 and MRR on the golden set, with and without it.”
