The problem

"Play Console wants an AI assistant for app developers' user reviews. Popular apps get thousands of reviews a day in many languages, and developers can't read them all. The system should (1) tag each review with an issue category (crash, login, payments, performance, feature request…) and a sentiment, (2) give the developer a weekly 'top issues' summary with example reviews, and (3) draft replies the developer can edit and send. Design it, and tell me how you'd know it works."

This was asked by an interviewer playing a senior data scientist, so most of the grading is on measurement: the classifier choice, labels, metrics and the experiment. The pipes matter less.

Non-functional: cost per review must be tiny at 20M a day; replies are never sent without the developer; personal data in reviews stays protected; per-language quality, not just the average.

The one-minute picture

Follow three real-looking reviews through the tagging path. The first is easy, the second is unsure, and the third is hard enough that a person decides. Use the step buttons to pause on any stage.

What to notice: the expensive model sits beside the main path, not on it. Every review pays for the classifier (fractions of a cent per thousand); only the ones the gate rejects pay for the LLM. Raters see the smallest slice of all, and that slice is the most valuable training data you have, because it is exactly where the model is weakest.

Tagging 20M reviews a day: a classifier cascade

Sending every review to an LLM is the expensive default, not the design. At about 400 tokens in and 10 out, a mid-tier LLM costs roughly $2,900 a day (≈ $87K a month), and the 1.8B backfill would cost six figures. The labels are fixed and the volume is high, so this is a job for a small classifier.

  1. Distillation. An LLM labels about 50K reviews once, sampled across languages, apps and categories (oversampling rare ones like payments). Experts check a sample of those labels.
  2. Train a small multilingual classifier on them: a fine-tuned encoder (BERT-size), or embeddings plus logistic regression. One model for category, one head for sentiment.
  3. Confidence gate in code. If the top probability is low (for example below 0.7), the top two are close, or the predicted class is high-stakes (payments, security), the review goes to the LLM with the taxonomy and examples.
  4. Still unsure → raters. Their labels go back into training (active learning).
  5. Backfill runs the same classifier as a batch job over the 1.8B reviews: hours of GPU time instead of a large LLM bill.

Rough cost: classifier ≈ $20 to $50 a day of GPU, plus about 10% of reviews at LLM prices ≈ $300 a day, so about $10K a month instead of $87K. Tune the threshold so auto-tagged reviews meet the precision target and the share sent to the LLM fits the budget.

Turning the threshold knob

The confidence threshold is a business dial, not a model setting. Raise it and auto-tags get more precise, but more reviews go to the LLM and the bill rises. Lower it and you save money while more wrong tags reach the weekly summary. Pick it on the golden set, per class, so payments can demand more certainty than praise.

ThresholdSent to LLMAuto-tag precisionMonthly cost
0.5≈ 4%≈ 86%≈ $5K
0.7 (chosen)≈ 10%≈ 93%≈ $10K
0.9≈ 30%≈ 97%≈ $27K

Illustrative numbers. The point is the shape: the last few points of precision cost the most, so stop where the summary's error rate is good enough for developers to trust it.

Why not keywords first? Keywords fire on almost every review and are confidently wrong about 40% of the time, so they cannot decide which reviews are easy. The classifier's own confidence can.

Labels: building the eval set

The 5,000 old labels use the wrong taxonomy, and keyword tags cannot be ground truth because they are the 60% baseline. Build a new golden set:

  • Taxonomy and guidelines first: a written definition and examples for each category, including the confusing pairs (performance vs crash, login vs account).
  • Sample: stratified by category, language and app type, from recent production reviews. Oversample rare classes so each key class has 200 or more examples. About 3,000 to 5,000 reviews in total.
  • Raters: trained raters with the guidelines, native speakers for the top languages. Map old labels to the new taxonomy where they line up, to save time.
  • Quality: two raters label an overlap set; Cohen's kappa ≥ 0.7. Disagreements go to an adjudicator, and low agreement means the guidelines need fixing.
  • Leakage: golden-set reviews never go into training or into prompt examples.

Why 200 per class? With about 40 examples, a measured 90% recall is uncertain by roughly ±9 points; with 400 it is about ±3. Small slices give numbers you cannot trust.

Comparing gold and predicted labels is done in code. An LLM judge adds nothing for classification, because every item already has a gold label.

Weekly "top issues" summary

The weekly job is mostly SQL. The scene shows why the LLM appears only at the end: every number in the summary has already been computed and is checked again after the text is written.

  • Counts and trends come from SQL over the tags in BigQuery: per category, app version, country and language. The LLM never counts.
  • Top means rising: compare each tag's weekly count with its baseline (percentage change with a minimum volume, or a z-score) to flag spikes like "crash reports up 4× since version 3.2".
  • Dedupe before counting: near-duplicate and bot reviews (review bombing) should not look like a real spike.
  • New issue types: embed reviews tagged "other", cluster them, and let an LLM name the big clusters, for example "battery drain after update". Frequent ones become candidate categories.
  • The LLM writes the text from the SQL results plus 3 representative reviews per issue, and code checks that every number in the text matches the query.

Reply drafts

The LLM drafts a reply in the review's language, using the developer's own FAQ, release notes and past replies as grounding. The developer always edits or approves; nothing is auto-sent.

Worst caseGuard
Promises: "we'll refund you", "fixed next week"Code checks block commitments and dates not in the developer's notes
Invented facts about the appGround only in the developer's FAQ and release notes; otherwise say "we're looking into it"
Leaking personal dataPII detector on input and output
Wrong language or tone-deaf reply to an angry userLanguage check in code; tone rules and examples in the prompt; toxicity filter on output

Abusive reviews are still tagged and shown to the developer (flagged, not hidden), and no reply is drafted for them.

Proving the drafts work: the A/B test

The unit of randomisation is the developer, because a developer who sees drafts on some reviews will change how they reply to all of them. The scene runs the two groups side by side over the same weeks.

Reading the result: a lift on the primary metric is necessary but not enough. If ratings after a reply fall, or reports about replies rise, the drafts are making developers reply more but worse, and the guardrail blocks the launch even when p < 0.05.

  • Randomise by developer (or app), not by review, because one developer sees all their drafts. Half get drafts, half don't, at the same time. Before/after comparisons are confused by seasons and launches.
  • Primary metric: share of reviews replied to within 7 days. Secondary: median time to reply.
  • Guardrails: rating change after a reply, reports and complaints about replies, edit rate.
  • Size it before starting and run at least 2 full weeks; no peeking and stopping early. Ship if the primary lift is significant (p < 0.05) and guardrails hold.
  • Offline checks and shadow mode test quality, but only an A/B test can show developers reply more.

Monitoring and retraining

  • Data drift: language mix, review length and vocabulary change (a new country launch, new slang). Detect by comparing input distributions.
  • Concept drift: the meaning of a tag shifts (a new payment method brings new complaints). Detect by precision and recall falling on a fresh weekly labelled sample.
  • Track the tag mix, the share of low-confidence reviews and the share sent to the LLM. Retrain monthly, or when drift alerts fire, and gate every new model on the golden set (no slice down more than 1 point).

What the interviewer probed, and strong answers

ProbeStrong answer
"Would you send all 20M reviews to an LLM?"No. Distil into a small multilingual classifier; the LLM only for low-confidence cases. About $10K vs $87K a month.
"Why not accuracy?"Classes are imbalanced. Report per-class precision and recall, macro-F1, and recall targets on crash and payments.
"Your labels are on the old taxonomy. Now what?"New guidelines, stratified sample, trained raters, kappa on an overlap set, adjudication, 200+ per key class.
"Why an LLM judge for classification?"None needed: compare predicted and gold labels in code.
"Is 40 examples enough for 90% recall?"No, that is about ±9 points. Use 200 to 400 per key class.
"How do you prove drafts helped?"A/B test randomised by developer, run at the same time, one primary metric plus guardrails, sized in advance.
"What's the worst reply?"A false promise or invented fact. Developer approval, code checks on commitments, grounding in their own docs.

Saying it in an interview

"At 20 million reviews a day with fixed categories, I wouldn't call an LLM per review. I'd have an LLM label about 50K reviews once, train a small multilingual classifier on them, and send only the low-confidence ones to the LLM, with raters for what's still unsure, which also gives me new training data. For evaluation I'd build a new golden set with written guidelines, two raters and kappa of at least 0.7, with 200 or more examples per key class, and report per-class precision and recall and macro-F1, with recall of 90% or more on crash and payments, sliced by language. The weekly summary gets its counts and spikes from SQL, and the LLM only writes the text. Reply drafts are never auto-sent and are checked for promises. To prove they help, I'd run an A/B test randomised by developer, with the share of reviews replied to within 7 days as the primary metric and ratings and complaints as guardrails."
